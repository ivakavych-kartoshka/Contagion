\section{Family-Wise Control Extended to the Remaining Families}
\label{app:mult}

Appendix~\ref{app:bh} accounted for multiplicity among the five composition
cells; the topology, content-form, obfuscation and loop comparisons that the main
paper reports as outcomes were previously labelled exploratory without any
family-wise accounting. We now extend the same Benjamini--Hochberg control to
every null among those families that is testable from the stored artefact,
without any new model call (\texttt{scripts/familywise\_extended.py}).

Table~\ref{tab:mult} lists the $m = 12$ tests, the exact $p$-values (Fisher's
exact test on the observed counts; exact two-sided binomial against the
point prediction for the loop), the within-family BH threshold at $q = 0.05$,
and the verdict. Every family keeps at least one surviving rejection, and the
Claude floor comparison honestly fails (Section~\ref{app:tau} shows there is
almost nothing to defend). Three findings deserve to be stated explicitly:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The star--tree ASR gap is not significant.} At $n = 7$ (Llama,
  40 natural runs per cell) the tree reaches its target in $0.800$ of runs
  versus $0.725$ for the star; the difference is $p = 0.60$, within the noise the
  caption already flags. What \emph{is} supported---and survives the correction---
  is the chain--tree gap ($0.450$ vs.\ $0.800$, $p = 0.0024$) and the
  chain--star gap ($0.450$ vs.\ $0.725$, $p = 0.022$). We therefore read the
  main paper's ``most permissive'' as a \emph{numerical} ordering only, and the
  star--tree reversal with scale rests on the $n = 10$ and DeepSeek replicates
  (Section~\ref{app:topology}), not on the $n = 7$ gap alone.
  \item \textbf{The content-form effect survives anything we can correct for.}
  On Llama's middle edge the same target survives $29/30$ embedded versus
  $2/30$ canonical ($p = 2.3 \times 10^{-13}$).
  \item \textbf{The loop falsifications all survive.} The exact finite chain
  (Section~\ref{app:cyclic}) is rejected in every model against the observed
  last-round manager rate ($p \le 1.6\times 10^{-4}$ even for the marginal
  DeepSeek cell).
\end{enumerate}

A pooled, claim-level BH over all $22$ tests that yield a $p$-value anywhere in
the paper ($5$ composition + $5$ depth + $3$ topology + $1$ content form +
$5$ obfuscation + $3$ loop) leaves the same fourteen rejections as the
within-family passes: the composition family's Llama cell, the depth trends of
qwen and both Llama replicates, the two topology gaps named above, the
content-form effect, the four non-floor obfuscation tests, and the three loop
tests. Nothing that is claimed as significant in the main text is an artefact of
unchecked multiplicity, and nothing that fails here is claimed there. The $R_0$
point comparisons ($0.831$ vs.\ $0.821$ and $0.420$) remain descriptive because, as
the topology caption states, their numerator and denominator are not recoverable
from the per-cell files we archived, so a test on them would not be reproducible
from the release.

\section{Context-Clustered Interval on the Weak Edge}
\label{app:cluster}

The main paper's power and multiplicity paragraph reports that a homogeneity test
rejects exchangeability across benign task contexts on the weak Llama edge. Because
that violates the i.i.d.\ assumption behind Wilson intervals, we recompute the edge
rate with a cluster bootstrap that resamples \emph{repeats} (seed replications), not
individual trials. The test is the Pearson statistic on the
per-context counts ($18/56$, $3/56$, $13/48$ at $T = 0.7$, so $n = 160$ across three
contexts): $\chi^2 = 13.40$ on $\mathrm{df} = 2$, with the tail evaluated by the
Wilson--Hilferty approximation used throughout the code, $p = 0.0014$ (the exact
$\chi^2_2$ tail gives $0.0012$; the difference does not affect any conclusion). At a fixed
temperature ($T = 0.7$, six repeats of twenty trials) the two
intervals nearly coincide (Table~\ref{tab:cluster}): the design-effect widening is
$0.99\times$, so within a temperature the Wilson interval is well calibrated. The
overdispersion $\varphi = 2.93$ is a \emph{cross-temperature} artefact---temperature
is a deliberate systematic factor, not trial noise---which is why we compute
$\varphi$ within a temperature.

\section{Threshold Sensitivity of the Floor Effect}
\label{app:tau}

A referee may ask whether the ``resistant'' verdict on the frontier model is an
artefact of the compromise threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{ASV}} = 0.9$. We re-score the
single-hop obfuscation cells (Claude, $n = 30$ per cell) at
$\tau \in \{0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0\}$ directly from the stored ASV scores---a
deterministic re-thresholding, no new model calls. The compromise rate is
\emph{invariant} across $\tau$ in every cell (Table~\ref{tab:tau}): the no-defence
baseline stays at $0.17$ and redaction at $0.00$ regardless of the threshold. Note
that $0.17$ is the \emph{single-hop} no-defence rate for this model and cell; the
end-to-end chain rate quoted in the main paper's headroom discussion is $0.10$, a
different quantity with a different denominator, and the two are not
interchangeable. The
reason is that the ASV distribution is bimodal---of $180$ trials, $158$ score $0.0$
and $14$ score $1.0$; the only eight intermediate trials all score $\le 0.667$, i.e.\
below the lowest threshold tested. The floor effect is therefore a property of the
model, not of where the judge's cut is placed.

\section{Theory Assumptions: Independence, Correlation, and $R_0$}
\label{app:theory-assumptions}

This appendix clarifies the theoretical assumptions invoked in Section 3.8 of the
main paper.

\paragraph{Product-of-complements and correlation.} The percolation recurrence
\[
\Pr[v] = 1 - \prod_{u \to v}\!\bigl(1 - s_{u\to v}\,\Pr[u]\bigr)
\]
assumes \emph{edge activations} (and the corresponding parent-reach events relevant to the
product form) are conditionally independent given the graph structure. When multiple
parents $u_1,\ldots,u_k$ of $v$ share a common ancestor, the events $\{\Pr[u_i]\}$ may be
correlated, so $\Pr[\bigwedge_i (\text{not reached via }u_i)] \neq \prod_i (1 - s_{u_i\to v}\Pr[u_i])$
in general. For the specific topologies evaluated in this work (chains, trees, star,
and the small manager/worker loop), we do not rely on that product form across shared
ancestors; where we use it, we treat it as the marginal approximation under the stated
conditions or restrict claims to graph classes where the independence holds. For
arbitrary DAGs with shared ancestors, a path-based (inclusion--exclusion) formulation
or explicit correlation modelling is required.

\paragraph{$R_0$ definition and scope.} For strictly acyclic (feed-forward) graphs, the
standard linearized next-generation matrix $M$ with $M_{uv}=s_{u\to v}$ is strictly
triangular in a topological ordering, hence $\rho(M)=0$. This is a structural property of
acyclicity, not an empirical safety guarantee. We therefore report $R_0$ as a
\emph{descriptive, finite-horizon} reproduction statistic: specifically, we define
$R_0$ as the mean number of newly compromised neighbours produced per compromised agent
instance over the observed finite horizon (generation convention as stated in
Section 4.2 of the main paper), with its denominator clearly specified. We do not
interpret $\rho(M)<1$ as a threshold for safety on finite acyclic graphs; reachability
is given by the percolation (Eq.~3 in the main paper), not by $R_0$ alone.

\paragraph{Parallel paths and defence placement.} Hardening a single node is not
equally effective when there exist multiple $s$-$t$ paths: effectiveness depends on
whether that node lies on all paths (a cut). For unique-path graphs the effect is
path-specific; we do not generalise equal effectiveness to multi-path DAGs. Placement
claims are limited to the graph classes we evaluate.

\paragraph{Transportability and product composition.} The chain identity
$\mathrm{ASR}=\prod_i s_i^{\mathrm{nat}}$ is algebraic given the Markov/chain
structure. However, composing \emph{isolated} per-edge estimates $s^{\mathrm{ctrl}}$
requires that $s^{\mathrm{ctrl}}_i \approx s^{\mathrm{nat}}_i$ (transportability). We do
not assume this holds in general; we test it empirically and use the depth-error slope
as a diagnostic (Section 5.2 of the main paper).

\section{Recurrence Makes the Threshold Non-Vacuous}
\label{app:cyclic}

This appendix gives the full treatment of the recurrent (cyclic) case summarised in
Section~5.8 of the main paper. We test a pattern real frameworks use: a manager
broadcasting to workers that report back. (The complementary test of the percolation
recurrence on a reconvergent \emph{acyclic} graph, where it can fail, is
Appendix~\ref{app:reconvergent}.)

For a manager with $w$ reporting workers, the infection matrix has
$M[m\!\to\! w_j] = a_j$ and $M[w_j\!\to\! m] = c_j$, and its characteristic
polynomial is $\lambda^{w-1}(\lambda^2 - \sum_j a_j c_j)$. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \rho(M) = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_j a_j\,c_j}
  \label{eq:cyclicrho}
\end{equation}
for every $w$ (checked numerically against the eigenvalues to machine precision on
$320$ random configurations). Two consequences follow. A feed-forward reading sets
every $c_j = 0$, so $\rho = 0$ however strong the edges are---the vacuity of
Section~3.8 of the main paper again, now with the feedback edges removed. And the
system is supercritical exactly when $\sum_j a_j c_j > 1$; for symmetric edges
$a_j = c_j = s$ this is $w\,s^2 > 1$. That is a \emph{design rule} rather than a
diagnostic: at $s = 0.5$ a manager needs five reporting workers to sustain an
endemic injection, at $s = 0.7$ three, at $s = 0.9$ two.

Applied to measured survivals, Eq.~\eqref{eq:cyclicrho} makes opposite
predictions, which is the test a criterion has to pass to be worth using
(Table~\ref{tab:cyclic}). On Llama the sum is $1.800$ and on DeepSeek $1.018$, so both
frontier loops should be supercritical; on qwen2.5:7b it is $0.578$, so the same loop
should decay.

The measured dynamics follow in \emph{direction}: on Llama the recurrent manager is
re-compromised by its own workers' reports in $0.725$ $[0.572, 0.839]$ of runs and does
not die out---at round five it is still compromised in $0.700$ of runs with the workers
near saturation ($0.925$, $0.975$); extending the loop to eight rounds leaves it endemic
(manager $0.583$, workers $0.900$). DeepSeek, whose $\sum_j a_jc_j = 1.018$ sits just
above criticality, behaves the same way---the manager settles at $0.567$ with workers at
$0.767$ and $0.700$ rather than decaying---so the recurrent verdict is not a
single-model artefact. On qwen the same loop decays: the manager's re-compromise rate
falls from $0.775$ $[0.625, 0.877]$ to $0.650$ and the workers plateau ($0.475$,
$0.525$).
A replication with three workers (versus two) at $60$ trials on Llama gives
$\rho = 1.351$ (supercritical) and manager re-compromise $0.683$ $[0.558, 0.787]$ at
round three, remaining endemic at $0.583$ by round eight---confirming the supercritical
verdict across worker counts.



\paragraph{The exact finite chain does not reproduce those rates.} The mean-field
comparison above says only which side of criticality a loop is on, and we do not read it
as a model of the per-round process. Building the exact finite chain instead---with
$w$ workers, the manager compromised by at least one returning report, and workers able
to be compromised only through the manager---gives
$m_{k+1} = 1 - \prod_j (1 - a_j c_j m_k)$, which is strictly below one at every round
even when $\sum_j a_jc_j > 1$. Table~\ref{tab:cyclic-markov} compares that prediction
with the observed manager rate round by round.

The gaps are far outside sampling error (at $n = 40$--$60$ the standard error of an
observed rate is $\approx 0.06$--$0.08$), and their \emph{sign} differs by model, so the
discrepancy is not a uniform bias that a single correction would absorb. The
diagnostic is visible in the round-by-round data: after Llama's manager falls to
$0.725$, its workers remain at $0.925$ and $0.975$ where the memoryless chain puts them
at the manager's own rate, and on DeepSeek the workers are compromised \emph{more} often
inside the loop ($0.767$) than their isolated per-edge rate ($0.650$) implies. Both
patterns say that a binary per-round state is not sufficient here either---the same
conclusion the content-form ablation reaches for the chain---so we report the loop as a
directional mean-field prediction and state explicitly that the finite-chain
formulation, as written, is falsified by our measurements rather than supported by them.

Two caveats. First, $\rho$ is computed from \emph{isolated} survivals that
Sections~5.2--5.7 of the main paper show to be unreliable; the Llama and
qwen verdicts survive because neither $\sum_j a_jc_j$ is marginal ($1.80$, $0.58$),
whereas DeepSeek's $1.018$ sits close enough to the critical value that its
supercritical label should be read together with its measured dynamics (endemic over
eight rounds) rather than from the point estimate alone. Second, we study a single
recurrent pattern at one temperature; the three models span five to eight rounds,
enough to separate decay from saturation, not to demonstrate extinction.


\section{Judge Calibration, Scope, and Known Failure Modes}
\label{app:judge-validation}

\paragraph{What was measured.} The compromise rule of Section~3.4 of the main paper
($C = \ind[\mathrm{ASV} \ge 0.9]$, ASV $=$ fraction of target bigrams present) was
calibrated on \textbf{ground-truth-labelled} outputs from the local model

\paragraph{Scope of this validation, and what it does not establish.} The calibration
covers \textbf{one model} (\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}), \textbf{one payload family}
(exact-string targets), and \textbf{no defence}: both the benign and the compromised
classes are drawn from undefended runs. The rule is deterministic and needs no
per-model recalibration, but we do \emph{not} claim that the same operating point
transfers unchanged to frontier models at temperature $0.7$, to paraphrase- or
obfuscation-obfuscated targets, or to defended runs. Two further failure modes follow
from the operational definition and should be read alongside every number we report:
(i) a paraphrased leak that destroys the target's bigrams is scored as
\emph{not} propagating (false negative), and (ii) a safe quotation of the target
inside a benign summary is scored as propagating (false positive). Validation on
frontier models, multiple payloads and defended conditions requires human
annotation of a stratified subset across those conditions; we report the protocol
for it below so the limitation is precise rather than open-ended. Read against a
human annotator, both failure modes concern the \emph{semantics} of the target: a
paraphrase that destroys its bigrams is scored as not propagating, and a safe
quotation inside a benign summary is scored as propagating.

\paragraph{Outcome levels.} Every judged activation is reported at level (a)
\emph{textual propagation}---the target string/code fragments appear. The calibration
set contains no case that separates (a) from (b) \emph{instruction
adoption/compliance} within the same output, and no case of (c) \emph{harmful
action} (the payload is a benign code, so (c) is by construction outside this
study). Adoption is a subset of propagation in this task family: an agent that acts
on the injected instruction must surface the target, whereas the converse does not
hold. Since every label here is positive on (a) whenever it is positive on (b), the
judge is an \emph{upper bound} on the adoption rate and we report (a) as the
propagation outcome. We do not use it as evidence of instruction adoption or of
harmful action anywhere in the paper.

\paragraph{Replay procedure.} In the same spirit we state precisely what the
content-form replay of Section~5.4 of the main paper does. Each replay trial draws a
fresh receiver, rotates the benign assignment over the same pool, and consumes either
content captured from a real natural run, a canonical artefact produced on demand, a
fixed hand-written string, or the in-context content under a different sender label;
temperature, token budget, system prompt, role, judge and thresholds are identical
across arms, so the arms differ only in message form and in the label of its sender.
The in-context arm is the harness self-check and has a target it must hit, namely the
rate measured in-chain; the other arms are read as comparisons against it. Because
each trial resamples the source output and draws the receiver fresh, a replay rate can
differ from an in-chain rate through sampling noise and per-context variation as well
as through message form. On the edge where the self-check misses ($0.733$ versus
$0.947$) we therefore report the value for completeness and do not use it as an
estimate of the form effect. The local re-run of this harness
(\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}, $4$ natural trials and $2$ replays per arm and edge) is
consistent with that reading rather than with a validated harness: every edge sits
inside the replay confidence interval, yet two of three edges differ from the in-chain
rate by $0.500$, which at this sample size the interval cannot resolve. We read the
local run as \emph{underpowered}, not as a passed self-check, and we do not report it
as evidence about form; the $n = 30$--$40$ configuration behind the content-form table
of the main paper (its Table~4) remains the only configuration in which the
self-check discriminates.

\section{Additional Validation: Second Payload and Extended Depth Curves}
\label{app:extended-validation}

\paragraph{Second payload (MANGO-42).} To address whether the main results are artefacts
of a single payload, we replicated the content-form probe on Llama 3.3 70B with a second
target (\texttt{MANGO-42}, semantically unrelated to \texttt{BANANA-77}), on the same
$n = 4$ chain with the same judge and assignment. The direction of every effect survives.
Chain survival is $1.000/1.000/0.925$ against \texttt{BANANA-77}'s $1.000/0.875/0.971$,
so the chain reaches further with the second payload rather than less. The content-form
effect on the middle edge persists and is in the same direction ($0.933$ in-context
versus $0.767$ canonical, a $1.22\times$ gap where the first payload showed
$14\times$), which is what one expects if the mechanism is packaging rather than a
property of one string. Composition is \emph{not} preserved: here the chain product
under-estimates the measured rate by $\Delta = +0.258$ $[+0.075, +0.442]$, bootstrap
$p = 0.006$, so the second payload produces reinforcement (super-Markov) where the first
produced a large under-estimate. We report that asymmetry rather than smoothing it: the
sign of the composition error is payload-dependent too, which reinforces the paper's
position that transportability is an empirical question and not a property of the
protocol. The second payload therefore confirms that the form effect and the per-edge
heterogeneity are not artefacts of \texttt{BANANA-77}, while the transport failure
changes sign.

\paragraph{Extended depth curves.} To test whether the depth-error slope diagnostic holds
beyond the $n = 4$--$8$ chains of the main results, we measured Llama 3.3 70B at
$n = 15$ agents ($120$ natural runs, $60$ controlled trials per edge). The Spearman rank
correlation $\rho = +0.76$ (permutation $p = 0.014$) and OLS slope $+6.8$ percentage
points per hop reproduce the $n = 8$ pattern (slope $+6.3$); the chain dies at depth $11$
($P(C_{11}=1) = 0.000$ $[0.000, 0.031]$), which sets the operational limit for this
measurement. The depth-by-depth relative error rises monotonically from $0\%$ at the entry
to $92\%$ at depth $10$, confirming that the slope is not an artefact of short chains.
These results validate that the transportability diagnostic remains interpretable at
longer chain depths, within the range where the payload survives at all.

\section{Practical Implications, in Full}
\label{app:discussion}

The four prescriptions of the Introduction change what is \emph{reported} rather than
what is built, and each addresses a specific measurement failure our results expose.

\paragraph{Composition is safe once transportability is checked.} Because the chain
identity is algebraic rather than a modelling choice, the product of in-chain per-hop
rates always equals end-to-end ASR; the only step that can fail---and that single-agent
benchmarks implicitly rely upon---is transporting isolated per-hop estimates into that
product. A single long chain measured under both protocols is therefore a cheap
diagnostic, and its \emph{slope} is the reading: shallow (DeepSeek, $+2.3$ points per
hop at $n = 10$) means isolated measurement is safe to compose, steep (qwen $+11.8$;
Llama $+6.8$) means the in-chain regime must be measured directly. Any composition of
isolated estimates should also state the graph assumptions it needs (no unmodelled
shared ancestors or correlations that invalidate the product-of-complements form) and
be supported by a transport check for the target graph class. The corollary for attack
design is that re-packaging is cheap for the defender to lose and cheap for the
attacker to use.

\paragraph{Defence evaluation requires headroom.} A defence cannot remove more signal
than its model permits: where the unprotected base rate is $0.10$ (Claude Sonnet 4.5)
redaction looks perfect because there is almost nothing to defend, while where it is
$1.00$ (Llama, susceptible cells) the same code is essential. Reporting a post-defence
ASR without its no-defence baseline confounds the defence effect with the model's
floor, and the utility measurements add a second dimension: semantic paraphrase is
strictly dominated in every cell we measured (Appendix~\ref{app:utility}).

\paragraph{Graph structure decides placement, not per-hop survival.} In a feed-forward
network the set of nodes that can affect end-to-end propagation is exactly the set on
some entry--target path, and within that set every node is equally effective
\emph{only} when a single cut covers the graph---that is, when no two entry--target
paths are disjoint. The practitioner's question is therefore: compute the paths, and
among the nodes they contain pick the one with lowest instrumentation cost. Per-hop
survival answers only the prior question of whether deploying a defence is worthwhile
at all; redundant paths are what promote placement to a genuine optimisation problem.

\paragraph{Worked example: an AutoGen/MetaGPT-style pipeline.} R4 Q1 asks for a worked
example in which the four prescriptions change an engineering decision that an aggregate
ASR alone would not. Consider the ``software-company'' pattern of MetaGPT or an AutoGen
group chat wired as planner $\to$ worker $\to$ reviewer $\to$ aggregator, with a content
filter to be placed at one hand-off. A single end-to-end ASR distorts this decision in
three concrete ways, all measurable from the numbers reported in the main paper.

\emph{Prescription 1 (do not compose isolated estimates):} suppose the team benchmarks
the worker role in isolation, as a single-agent injection benchmark would, and obtains
the Llama weak-edge value $\scites = 0.233$. Composing it with the other two edges gives
$\prod_i \scite{i} = 0.233$, and the team concludes the attack dies early and needs no
defence. The measured chain ASR is $0.850$, because inside the network the same edge
survives $\snats = 0.875$ (a $3.75\times$ underestimate); the depth-error slope would
have flagged exactly this kind of non-transport before deployment.

\emph{Prescription 4 (placement is structural):} on the chain every interior node lies
on the single entry--target path, so a filter at the reviewer is as effective as at the
worker (each brings predicted ASR to $0.000$). On a fan-in star, however, a hardened
branch node is useless while the centre (the node on every path) is decisive. The
aggregate ASR cannot tell these apart; only paths and cut sets can.

\emph{Prescription 3 (R$_0$ is not a safety criterion):} the seven-agent chain has
$R_0 = 0.821$ and the star $R_0 = 0.420$, both ``subcritical'', yet the target is
reached in $0.450$ and $0.725$ of runs respectively. Relying on $R_0 < 1$ would licence
skipping the filter on the star---the topology where the attack reaches the target most
often.

The aggregate ASR answers ``did this run fail?''; the per-hop transport check, the
structural placement view and the no-defence baseline together answer ``where, and
where should we intervene?''---which is the question that changes the engineering
decision.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity, in Full}
\label{app:limits}

\emph{Utility is a contamination proxy, not a correctness oracle}: the metric flags
whether the injected target string appears in the final deliverable, not whether the
legitimate task was answered correctly, and a task family with independently
verifiable ground truth (arithmetic or code execution) would sharpen the trade-off and
make paraphrase's cost comparable across conditions.

\emph{Transportability failures are real but their prevalence is unknown.} The
clearest failure is Llama's weak edge, powered to $\Delta = 0.615$, $p = 0.0002$,
$\mathrm{MDE} = 0.11$ at $n = 200$: isolation underestimates in-chain survival by
$3.75\times$. Nova shows the same middle-edge signature and DeepSeek transports
cleanly under the same test, so this covers three of our five models on one attack
family and one chain depth; how often the failure occurs elsewhere is uncharacterised,
and the depth-error slope is a diagnostic rather than a guarantee, since a short chain
at low power can read as flat.

\emph{Protocol sensitivity is reported rather than hidden.} Our initial significant
composition failure on DeepSeek was an artefact of the fixed-artefact policy and
vanished when artefacts were drawn fresh per trial ($p = 0.0030 \to p = 0.930$,
Appendix~A). The sensitivity flipped the sign of the deviation, so unstated protocol
choices that alter a result are a class of threat that benchmark replication
exercises routinely miss.

\emph{Scale, topology and defence coverage.} Headline chain results span four to
fifteen agents; star and tree cells are replicated across three models with two Llama
instances ($n = 7$ and $n = 10$); the recurrent case covers three models with limited
power. Configurations we do not vary include networks of tens of agents, mesh, debate
or mixture-of-experts topologies, re-injection, and defence families beyond redaction
and paraphrase---detection layers, hop isolation and structured queries are referenced
but not evaluated. Cell sizes of $30$--$40$ trials give $\mathrm{MDE} \approx 0.26$,
and the ASV threshold applies to a literal target that would need recalibration for
paraphrase-obfuscated ones.

The two readings are close ($0.186$ versus $0.151$, a difference of $0.035$ on a
$[0,1]$ scale), and they are not directly comparable in count: the position reading
uses every shared depth, whereas the role reading can only use roles that occur in
both orderings, and the duplicated role at the tail of a five-agent cycle reduces it
to three pairs. The informative quantity is therefore not which mean is smaller but
the size of the displacement itself: permuting the labels moves the
\emph{depth profile} by $0.186$ on average, with the second edge moving from $0.882$
to $0.381$. Two consequences follow, and the main paper is written to respect both.
First, the profile is \emph{not} invariant to the role labels, so the role assignment
cannot be ignored. Second, a single permutation cannot separate role from position:
with near-equal distances and three to four pairs, claiming that survival follows the
role rather than the depth would over-read the control. We therefore treat the
role profile of the main paper (its Fig.~2) as a profile under one fixed assignment
(planner, worker, reviewer, aggregator) rather than as a role effect, and we do not
use it to rank roles.

Llama~3.3~70B was the intended second half of the comparison, and the Bedrock
credential available to us expired before it could be run, so the reviewer's request to
repeat this for both models is only half answered. And at $n = 40$ runs per ordering the
per-edge standard error is roughly $0.07$--$0.08$, comparable to the displacement we
report, so a larger set of permutations would be needed to estimate a role effect even
if one exists.

\section{Testing Eq.~(3) Where It Can Fail: a Reconvergent DAG}
\label{app:reconvergent}

On the chain, the balanced tree and the single-entry fan-in star, each node has
effectively one entry--target path, so the percolation recurrence of Eq.~(3) collapses
to the chain identity of Eq.~(1) and cannot be tested. We therefore ran the harness on
a reconvergent DAG in which the two parents of the target share an upstream node,
$\texttt{a0} \!\to\! \texttt{a1} \!\to\! \{\texttt{a2}, \texttt{a3}\} \!\to\! \texttt{a4}$,
which is the case the recurrence is not guaranteed to satisfy when the parent-reach
events are correlated. The run used the local model \texttt{qwen2.5:7b} at $T = 0.7$,
40 natural runs and 30 replays per arm and edge (600 judged activations, 240 of them at
the target).

Two readings follow and must not be conflated. First, the recurrence built from the
\emph{in-chain} marginals underestimates the measured rate by $0.247$, which is what
one expects if the two parent-reach events are positively correlated; the harness's own
self-check passes at the target ($0.717$ replayed versus $0.792$ measured,
$\Delta = -0.075$), so the gap is not an artefact of a failed replay. Second, and more
consequential for practice, the recurrence built from the \emph{isolated} marginals
gives $0.309$ against a measured $0.792$: a single-agent benchmark that measures each
edge in isolation and composes the results underestimates reachability on this graph by
roughly $2.6\times$, and the per-edge discrepancies are concentrated
($\texttt{a1}\!\to\!\texttt{a3}$: $0.800$ in-chain versus $0.367$ isolated;
$\texttt{a2}\!\to\!\texttt{a4}$: $1.000$ versus $0.533$). The direction matches the
Llama chain failure of the main paper: isolation \emph{under}-estimates what a hand-off
achieves once the payload arrives inside an agent's work product. Two limits of this
measurement: three of the five edges do not reproduce their in-chain rate under replay
(differences of $0.14$--$0.30$ at $n = 30$ per arm), so the in-chain marginals carry
that uncertainty; and the result is one model on one small DAG, so we read it as
evidence that the recurrence is not exact in general rather than as a calibrated
correction.
